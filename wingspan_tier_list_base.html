Wingspan Bird Tier List: Base Game

This is a tier list of the 180 birds in the Wingspan base game, ranked by a computer player. The ten Swift-Start promo birds are included. The computer player taught itself the base game with no strategy advice from people, and then played 180,000 three-player games against copies of itself. We looked at every bird a player got blind, dealt into the starting hand or drawn off the deck, and asked one question: did the players who got that bird end up further ahead than the players who got some other bird? The answer, in points, is the number under each card.

This list covers the base game only. For every bird in the digital edition, expansions and promo packs included, see the all-expansions tier list.

The number is the bird's worth in points. It measures the change in your final lead over your best opponent (your score minus theirs) when you get this bird in place of an average one. Nobody chooses the top card of the deck, so across thousands of games everything else washes out and the difference belongs to the bird.

Values by round. Each bird has five values: one for the starting hand and one for each round. In these games the computer kept each starting bird on a coin flip, so luck decides which birds a player gets there too. Franklin's Gull is worth +13.2 points in the starting hand, +2.2 in round 2 and −0.2 in round 4. The number on the card blends the five, leaning on rounds 1 and 2 because players gain the most birds then. Tap a card for all five.

Based on 180,000 games, all played by the computer against copies of itself.

Surprises

  • Only half of the famous "Power 4" live up to the name. Franklin's Gull and Killdeer are among the six best birds in the base game. Common Raven and Chihuahuan Raven come out only a little above average.
  • Birds that tuck a card and draw a card are excellent. Barn Swallow is the best bird in the base game, and American Robin and Purple Martin are in the top four with it.
  • Birds that draw bonus cards are good, and best late. 11 of the 15 are above average, and the four best birds to draw in round 4 all have that power.
  • Giving every player the same thing is costly. The five lowest birds in the base game all have a power of that kind.
  • The starting hand decides a lot. Franklin's Gull and Killdeer are each worth about +13 points in the starting hand, and no bird drawn in round 4 is worth as much as a point and a half.

The computer player and popular opinion

  • Only half of the "Power 4" are as strong as people say. Common Raven, Chihuahuan Raven, Franklin's Gull and Killdeer top the King Panda Games base-game list and many players' own lists. The computer agrees about two of them. Franklin's Gull (+3.7) is the third-best bird in the base game and Killdeer (+3.0) is the sixth. The two ravens come out at +0.5 each, 37th and 38th of the 180 birds, in B tier. The computer plays each raven about 15 times per 100 games, half as often as Franklin's Gull. Their cost of three food seems to be the trouble. American Crow turns an egg into one food and costs a single food, and at +1.6 it beats both ravens. Nobody can promise the computer plays perfectly, and a self-taught player may miss some of the ways strong players use a raven. But it plays well, and in these games the ravens are only a little better than an average bird.
  • Wood Duck looks overrated in the base game. Wingsplain's tier list puts Wood Duck in its top tier, and Board Game Arena forum posters call it one of the strongest cards. Here it comes out almost exactly average, 71st of the 180 birds, and the computer plays it about 8 times per 100 games. Its cost of three food seems to be the trouble. Common Yellowthroat, Pied-Billed Grebe and Ruddy Duck have the same power for a single food, and all three are above average. In the starting hand each of them is worth more than +4.5 points, twice as much as Wood Duck (+2.2). Nobody can promise the computer plays perfectly, but in the base game the cheap versions look like the better cards.

Patterns across the whole deck

  • Cheap birds are best. The average value falls with every food in the cost, from +1.4 points for the three birds that cost no food to −0.3 for birds that cost three.
  • Point bombs are late cards. The 32 birds worth 6 points or more average −0.2 points overall. In the starting hand they sit more than a point below average as a group, and not one of them is clearly above average there. By round 4, 27 of the 32 are above average. Small birds run the other way. Birds worth 0 to 2 points average +1.8 in the starting hand and −0.2 in round 4.
  • A bird's power is its most important quality. Points, food cost, egg limit, habitats, nest and wingspan together explain only about a quarter of the differences between birds. The power explains the rest.
  • Pink birds are the best color early. Pink birds are worth more than a point in the starting hand, a point in round 1, and nothing by round 3. White birds are the weakest color in the starting hand, and the only color that gains value as the game goes on.
  • Two habitats beat one. Birds that live in two habitats average +0.8 points, and birds limited to one average −0.2. All six of the best birds live in grassland and one other habitat. A third habitat adds nothing, and birds that live in all three average −0.1.
  • Star nests are best. Birds whose nest counts as any type average +1.2 points, and 14 of the 17 are above average. Several of the best birds have one, though, so the nest may simply come with a strong power.
  • The Swift-Start promo birds are ordinary. The ten of them average −0.2 points, and four are above average. White-Throated Swift (+0.9) is the best and the only one in A tier. Black-Chinned Hummingbird (−1.1) is the third-lowest bird in the list, and Canvasback (−0.8) is weak too. Both of those give something to every player.

Power families

  • Tuck and draw is the best engine. Brown birds that tuck a card from your hand and then draw a card average +2.0 points, and every one of them but Tree Swallow is above average. Barn Swallow (+4.4) is the best bird in the base game, and American Robin and Purple Martin are in the top four with it.
  • Free food from the supply pays. Spotted Towhee, the fifth-best bird, gains a seed from the supply, and every chickadee and nuthatch that caches a seed from the supply is a little above average. Taking a food from the birdfeeder "if available" is much weaker, and all ten of those birds are below average.
  • Spending food to tuck cards loses. The five birds that discard a food to tuck 2 cards from the deck are all below average, and all five sit in the bottom quarter of the list.
  • Free dice are gold. Black Vulture, Black-Billed Magpie and Turkey Vulture, the pink birds that take a die when another player's predator succeeds, are all in the top 20. The pink birds that lay an egg when another player lays eggs are all close to average.
  • "All players" powers hurt. Of the 14 brown birds whose power gives every player the same thing, only Baltimore Oriole and Eastern Phoebe are above average. The five lowest birds in the base game all have a power of this kind, counting Ruby-Throated Hummingbird, which gives every player a die. The computer plays those five often, 26 to 38 times per 100 games, so their low values don't come from sitting in hand. The gift to two opponents seems to cost more than the power gives back. The three birds that reward the player with the fewest birds in a habitat are below average too.
  • "Play an additional bird" powers are weak. Only Red-Eyed Vireo among the ten of them is above average, and House Wren is among the twenty lowest birds in the base game. Players prize the saved action, but at three players the computer plays eight of the ten less often than a typical bird.
  • Bonus-card birds are good, and best late. 11 of the 15 birds that draw bonus cards are above average, led by Atlantic Puffin and Bell's Vireo at +1.3 each. The four below average are the four that live only in grassland. As a group these birds are below average in the starting hand and gain value as the game goes on. Whooping Crane, Atlantic Puffin, Bell's Vireo and Wood Stork are the four best birds to draw in round 4.
  • Some powers go unplayed. Birds that move themselves to another habitat and birds that repeat another bird's power are all below average, and the computer plays each of them less than 3 times per 100 games. Their low values mostly come from the computer declining to play them, and a player with a good brown power to repeat may do better with them. Birds played less than once per 100 games carry a grey "rarely played" flag in the list, and their order among themselves means little.

Nearly identical birds

  • Extra habitats add value. Violet-Green Swallow (any habitat) beats Tree Swallow (wetland only) by nearly 2 points with the same power, printed points, nest and number of food. Dark-Eyed Junco (forest or grassland) beats Pine Siskin (forest only) by more than 2 points with the same power, printed points and number of food.
  • A cheaper cost adds value. The five birds that draw 2 cards and discard 1 at the end of the turn show it best. The three that cost one food are worth +0.4 to +0.6. Red-Breasted Merganser, at two food, is worth +0.2, and Wood Duck, at three food, is almost exactly average.

Timing

  • The starting hand matters most. Birds differ about eight times as much in value in the starting hand as when drawn in round 4. Franklin's Gull in the starting hand is worth +13.2 points, Killdeer +13.0 and Spotted Towhee +8.0. No bird drawn in round 4 is worth as much as a point and a half, and none costs even half a point.
  • Keep two or three birds. In these games the computer kept each starting bird on a coin flip, so luck set the number of birds kept. Players who kept two or three birds did best, and they finished about a point better off than players who kept none or all five. Those birds were kept at random, so a player who picks well may want a different number. Against the average starting food, a kept invertebrate is worth +0.8 points and a seed +0.3. Fish (−0.2) and fruit (−0.3) are a little below average, and rodent (−0.6) is the worst food to keep.
  • Late in the game, take the points. The four best birds to draw in round 4 all draw bonus cards, and three of them are worth 6 points or more. Blue-Winged Warbler, an 8-point bird with no power at all, is fifth.

Fine print

  • Small differences don't mean much. Each value is good to about ±0.10 points, so birds a few tenths apart are effectively tied. Splitting the games into two halves and rating the birds in each half separately gives nearly the same list. The two sets of values have a correlation above 0.99, where 1 would be a perfect match.
  • Tray picks are left out. The numbers come only from birds that arrive face down: dealt into the starting hand, or drawn from the deck by the draw-cards action or by a bird's power. A bird taken from the tray is a choice, so the players who take it aren't a fair comparison.
  • Each game result is compared with a forecast. Throughout a game, the computer keeps an estimate of each player's final lead or deficit against their best opponent. A bird is judged by the gap between the real result and that estimate as it stood just before the bird came off the deck. The estimate is made before anyone sees the card, so it can't favor any bird, and it stops a player who was already far ahead from making the card they happened to draw look good.
  • Starting birds were kept on a coin flip. In these games the computer kept or discarded each starting bird at random. A bird's starting-hand value compares the players who kept it with the players who kept other birds, so the computer's own taste in starting birds plays no part in it.
  • A strong computer player's values. These come from 180,000 three-player games of the computer player against copies of itself, and it averaged 91 points a game. It learned the base game on its own, with no strategy advice from people. Nobody can promise it plays perfectly, but it plays well, and its disagreements with popular opinion deserve a hearing. In these games it also kept its starting birds on a coin flip, made one tray pick in five at random, and one time in fifty took a random action or played a random bird, so it played a little below its usual level.
  • Three-player games of the base game, with no expansions. Birds whose worth depends on the number of opponents, pink powers for example, could rank differently at two or four players.
