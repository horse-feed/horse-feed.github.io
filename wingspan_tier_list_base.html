Wingspan Bird Tier List: Base Game

This is a tier list of the 170 birds in the Wingspan base game, ranked by a computer player. The computer player taught itself the base game with no strategy advice from people, and then played 120,000 three-player games against copies of itself. We looked at every bird a player got blind, dealt into the starting hand or drawn off the deck, and asked one question: did the players who got that bird end up further ahead than the players who got some other bird? The answer, in points, is the number under each card.

This list covers the base game only. For every bird in the digital edition, expansions and promo packs included, see the all-expansions tier list.

The number is the bird's worth in points. It measures the change in your final lead over your best opponent (your score minus theirs) when you get this bird in place of an average one. Nobody chooses the birds they are dealt or the top card of the deck, so across thousands of games everything else washes out and the difference belongs to the bird.

Values by round. Each bird has five values: one for the starting hand and one for each round. Franklin's Gull is worth +13.5 points when dealt into the starting hand, +2.0 in round 2 and nothing in round 4. The number on the card blends the five, leaning on the starting hand because players see the most birds then. Tap a card for all five.

Based on 120,000 games, all played by the computer against copies of itself.

Surprises

  • The famous "Power 4" live up to their name. Franklin's Gull and Killdeer are the two best birds in the base game, and Chihuahuan Raven and Common Raven are in the top two tiers too.
  • Cheap birds with small points beat point bombs. Only 3 of the 32 birds worth 6 points or more are above average, and they only pay off late.
  • Birds that tuck a card and draw a card are excellent. Barn Swallow, American Robin and Purple Martin are three of the six best birds.
  • Birds that draw bonus cards are weak. Only 2 of the 15 are above average.
  • The starting hand decides a lot. Franklin's Gull is worth +13.5 points when dealt at the start, and no bird drawn in round 4 is worth more than about a point either way.

The computer player and popular opinion

  • The "Power 4" are as strong as people say. Common Raven, Chihuahuan Raven, Franklin's Gull and Killdeer top the King Panda Games base-game list and many players' own lists. The computer agrees. Franklin's Gull (+6.4) and Killdeer (+4.6) are the two best birds in the base game, Chihuahuan Raven is tenth, and Common Raven is in A tier. All four turn your own eggs into food or cards, and American Crow, with the same kind of power, is ninth.
  • Wood Duck looks overrated in the base game. Wingsplain's tier list puts Wood Duck in its top tier, and Board Game Arena forum posters call it one of the strongest cards. Here it comes out below average, and the computer plays it about twice per 100 games. Its cost of three food seems to be the trouble. Common Yellowthroat, Pied-Billed Grebe and Ruddy Duck have the same power for a single food, and all three are above average. Nobody can promise the computer plays perfectly, but in the base game the cheap versions look like the better cards.

Patterns across the whole deck

  • Cheap birds are best. The average value falls with every food in the cost, from +1.6 points for the three birds that cost no food to −0.4 for birds that cost three.
  • Point bombs are late cards. Only 3 of the 32 birds worth 6 points or more are above average overall: Blue-Winged Warbler, Fish Crow and Greater Roadrunner. Big birds are among the weakest cards in the starting hand, and by round 4 nearly all of them are above average. Small birds run the other way. Birds worth 0 to 2 points average +1.7 in the starting hand and −0.4 in round 4.
  • A bird's power is its most important quality. Points, food cost, egg limit, habitats and wingspan together explain only about a sixth of the differences between birds. The power explains the rest.
  • Pink birds are the best color early. Pink birds are worth about a point in the starting hand and in round 1, and nothing by round 3. White birds are the weakest color, and the only one that gains value as the game goes on.
  • Two habitats beat one. Birds that live in two habitats average +0.9 points, and birds limited to one average −0.2. All six of the best birds live in grassland and one other habitat.
  • Star nests are best. Birds whose nest counts as any type average +1.4 points, and 12 of the 16 are above average. Several of the best birds have one, though, so the nest may simply come with a strong power.

Power families

  • Tuck and draw is the best engine. Brown birds that tuck a card from your hand and then draw a card average +1.6 points, and every one of them but Tree Swallow is above average. Barn Swallow, American Robin and Purple Martin are three of the six best birds in the game.
  • Free food from the supply pays. Spotted Towhee, the third-best bird, gains a seed from the supply, and every chickadee and nuthatch that caches a seed from the supply is above average. Taking a food from the birdfeeder "if available" is much weaker, and only Red-Bellied Woodpecker among those birds is above average.
  • Spending food to tuck cards loses. The five birds that discard a food to tuck 2 cards from the deck are all below average.
  • Free dice are gold. Black Vulture, Black-Billed Magpie and Turkey Vulture, the pink birds that take a die when another player's predator succeeds, are all in the top 15. The pink birds that lay an egg when another player lays eggs are near or below average.
  • "All players" powers hurt. Of the 12 brown birds whose power gives every player the same thing, only Eastern Phoebe is above average, and the computer uses even that power only 62% of the time it is offered.
  • "Play an additional bird" powers are weak. Only Red-Eyed Vireo among them is above average, and Tufted Titmouse is the lowest bird in the base game. Players prize the saved action, but at three players the computer rarely finds these birds worth playing.
  • Bonus-card birds pay late. Only California Condor and Bell's Vireo among the 15 birds that draw bonus cards are above average overall. These birds are weak in the starting hand and gain value as the game goes on, and Whooping Crane and Bell's Vireo are among the four best birds to draw in round 4.
  • Some powers go unplayed. Birds that move themselves to another habitat, birds that repeat another bird's power, and birds that reward the player with the fewest birds in a habitat are all below average, and the computer seldom plays them. Their low values mostly come from the computer declining to play them, and a player with a good brown power to repeat may do better with them. Birds played less than once per 100 games carry a grey "rarely played" flag in the list, and their order among themselves means little.

Nearly identical birds

  • Extra habitats add value. Violet-Green Swallow (any habitat) beats Tree Swallow (wetland only) by nearly 2 points with the same power, printed points, nest and number of food. Chipping Sparrow (forest or grassland) beats Grasshopper Sparrow (grassland only) by nearly 2 points with the same power and cost.
  • A cheaper cost adds value. The four birds that draw 2 cards and discard 1 at the end of the turn show it best. The three that cost one food are above average, and Wood Duck, at three food, is well below.

Timing

  • The starting hand matters most. Birds differ about six times as much in value when dealt at the start as when drawn in round 4. Franklin's Gull dealt at the start is worth +13.5 points, Killdeer +10.8 and Spotted Towhee +7.3. No bird drawn in round 4 is worth more than about a point either way.
  • The computer keeps the good birds. It keeps nearly every above-average bird it is dealt, 48 of 51 at least nine times in ten, and it discards most of the rest. Tap a card for its share of deals kept.
  • Late in the game, take the points. The best birds to draw in round 4 are Blue-Winged Warbler and Hooded Warbler, two big birds with no power at all.
  • The computer spends eggs with care. It uses the power of Franklin's Gull and Killdeer (discard an egg to draw 2 cards) only about 63% of the time it is offered, and the egg-for-food powers of the ravens and crows 64% to 74% of the time.

Fine print

  • Small differences don't mean much. Each value is good to about ±0.15 points, so birds a few tenths apart are effectively tied. Splitting the games into two halves and rating the birds in each half separately gives nearly the same list. The two sets of values have a correlation of 0.99, where 1 would be a perfect match.
  • Tray picks are left out. The numbers come only from birds that arrive face down: dealt into the starting hand, or drawn from the deck by the draw-cards action or by a bird's power. A bird taken from the tray is a choice, so the players who take it aren't a fair comparison.
  • Each game result is compared with a forecast. Throughout a game, the computer keeps an estimate of each player's final lead or deficit against their best opponent. A bird is judged by the gap between the real result and that estimate as it stood just before the bird came off the deck. The estimate is made before anyone sees the card, so it can't favor any bird, and it stops a player who was already far ahead from making the card they happened to draw look good.
  • The starting-hand value includes the keep. The computer chose its own starting birds, so a bird's starting-hand value covers being dealt it, whether the computer kept it or not.
  • A strong computer player's values. These come from 120,000 three-player games of the computer player against copies of itself. It learned the base game on its own, with no strategy advice from people. Nobody can promise it plays perfectly, but it plays well, and its disagreements with popular opinion deserve a hearing.
  • Three-player games of the base game, with no expansions. Birds whose worth depends on the number of opponents, pink powers for example, could rank differently at two or four players.
